    Adaptive Testing with Self-Evaluation¶

    Special Key takeaway

    Existing evidence: Examinee self-selected difficulty, background information, historical scores, and free text may all provide information for CAT cold starts; gains are usually concentrated in very short tests, distribution tails, or examinee with accurate prior predictions. Core limitations: The impact of only personalizing the first question is often quickly corrected by subsequent CAT, and the continued use of too narrow or wrong priors may cause bias and false early stopping; the entire process of self-selected items does not steadily improve measurement efficiency. Unresolved issues: The most valuable research gap is not "whether self-information can be used", but how to calibrate the information generated by Examinee into a reliable distribution, and allow it to simultaneously improve real dynamic topic selection and stopping, while maintaining out-of-sample accuracy and uncertainty calibration.

    Traditional Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT) is awkward when selecting the first question: the system has not observed any answers yet, but must give a question first. A common approach is to first give the examinee the same initial ability value, and then choose the question with the most information at that position. This topic studies another route: **Use examinee's judgment of one's own status at the beginning of the test to provide CAT with a small amount of but potentially useful individual information. **

    Self-evaluation here is a broad concept, which can be self-reported ability or symptom level, or active selection of item difficulty. It does not presuppose that the examinee will be able to accurately evaluate himself; the real question is under what conditions this signal is useful, and at what cost if used incorrectly.

    Three types of adjacent but different methods¶

    method examinee what it offers How to use the system How long does control last?
    Self-adapted testing Choose the difficulty level of the next question each time Administer the item at the selected level Usually throughout the test
    Self-informed starting point Self-report level, or choose starting difficulty Set initial ability value or first question Only affects the beginning
    Informative prior questionnaire, historical scores or other auxiliary variables Construct individualized prior distribution Gradually updated with response evidence

    These three types of methods share an intuition: examinee or external sources may hold information that has not been observed by standard CAT. But they are not the same design. In particular, a distinction should be made between letting people choose each question and only letting people's judgment help the CAT get started, and then completely handing over to the standard algorithm.

    The main research line of this topic¶

    What we focus on is the second category and its interface with the third category: in low-risk psychometrics or adaptive learning scenarios, let the examinee provide self-evaluation on the first or previous \(k\) questions, and then return to the original CAT's ability updates, topic selection rules, and stopping rules.

    A minimal version can be written as:

    \[ s_i \longrightarrow \widehat{\theta}_i^{(0)} \longrightarrow \text{before}k\text{question} \longrightarrow \text{Standard CAT update and topic selection} \longrightarrow SE(\widehat{\theta}_i)\le \varepsilon. \]

    Among them, \(s_i\) represents examinee's self-reported result or difficulty selection, \(\widehat{\theta}_i^{(0)}\) represents the resulting initial ability estimate, \(SE\) represents standard error (standard error), and \(\varepsilon\) represents the original accuracy stop threshold. The core hypothesis is: if \(s_i\) contains information related to the true level, CAT can enter the item area suitable for the examinee faster, thereby achieving the same estimation accuracy with fewer items.

    Parts that have not been tested in the first paper

    "The number of questions will be shortened" is the hypothesis prepared to be tested in this topic, not the conclusion reached in the first paper below. Wise et al. (1991) used a fixed set of 20 questions and could not compare stopping time or test length.

    Intensive reading has been completed for all 14 local candidate documents on this topic that have full text, including eight core documents, three supplementary documents and three extended documents. The core literature answers the individualized starting point and prior, the supplementary literature explains the selection process of examinee, and the extended literature completes the overall evidence of hybrid startup, \(a\)-stratified item bank management, and Self-Adapted Testing and CAT.

    What have you read in the first article?¶

    A Comparison of Self-Adapted and Computer-Adaptive Tests A direct comparison of two 20-question tests:

    • Self-Adapted Testing (SA): The examinee chooses from six difficulty levels before each question;
    • Computerized Adaptive Testing (CA): The computer selects the next question based on previous performance;
    • Both groups received correctness feedback after each question.

    The paper found that the SA group had a higher average ability estimate and lower post-test state anxiety, but it took longer and had a larger ability estimate error variance. It proves that "subjective information mastered by examinee may affect the testing process" is worthy of study, and also exposes the problems of validity, accuracy and causal explanation.

    What have you read in the second article?¶

    Adjusting Computer Adaptive Test Starting Points to Conserve Item Pool Compares common moderate difficulty starting points, average grade starting points, and combined course and grade point average starting points. The supporting information determines only the first question and thereafter the same CAT is restored.

    The combined starting point of courses and average grades spreads the first questions over a wider difficulty range, reduces the concentrated exposure of intermediate items, and maintains ability correlation close to the uninformation starting point under most conditions. However, it did not steadily shorten the variable length CAT; in short tests with looser stopping standards, the average number of questions was about one to two more questions. Using the average score alone also creates an exposure spike at the high-difficulty end due to the large number of 4.0 self-reports, indicating that errors or accumulated auxiliary information may simply shift exposure from the middle of the item bank to the tail of the item bank.

    What have you read in the third article?¶

    Empirical Initialization of the Trait Estimator in Adaptive Testing Model the pre-test auxiliary variables as predictor variables of latent ability, and construct the individualized initial point or complete empirical prior accordingly. Instead of first estimating each person's \(\widehat\theta\) and then doing ordinary regression, the paper retains ability as a latent variable and jointly estimates the regression coefficient and prior variance from the complete item-by-item responses. The actual data example uses the logarithmic response time of the previous test to predict the ability of the later vocabulary test, but does not re-compare the question size, accuracy or exposure of the standard CAT and the individualized prior CAT, so it is a prior construction and estimation method paper, not a CAT efficiency experiment.

    What have you read in Part 4?¶

    Computer Adaptive Testing with Empirical Prior Information: A Gibbs Sampler Approach for Ability Estimation Put the empirical prior constructed by the background variables into the initialization and intermediate/final ability estimate of CAT at the same time, and continue to use the maximum amount of information to select topics after each update. Therefore, "not changing the topic selection formula" does not mean "not changing the actual topic selection path." Under correctly specified simulation priors, the full empirical prior mainly improves the estimation of the 5-question short test and extreme abilities; the semi-empirical condition that only changes the starting point is generally weaker. As the number of questions increases, the advantage shrinks, and the original table does not support that the empirical method has a lower RMSE at each ability point. The paper uses a fixed test length, and does not test whether the number of questions can be reduced with the same stopping accuracy, nor does it simulate error self-evaluation, different prior variance or item exposure.

    What have you read in the fifth article?¶

    Combining Text Mining of Long Constructed Responses and Item-Based Measures Let the examinee write down the traumatic experience and symptoms, obtain the individual score through the existing text classifier, then establish the normal prior of \(\theta_n\mid y_n\) and combine it with the IRT questionnaire likelihood. In a sample of 99 people, the classification accuracy of the complete 21 questions increased from 0.94 for IRT-only to 0.97 for Hybrid; the average posterior standard error of the text prior plus 17 questions was approximately equal to the complete 21 questions without text prior. However, the 21 questions are pre-arranged in the same order according to the amount of information at the common diagnosis cut-off point \(-0.15\). Priority does not drive personalized real-time topic selection; the paper also does not report the classification accuracy, coverage rate or error prior risk of the 17 question conditions.

    What have you read in Chapter 6?¶

    Empirical Priors in Polytomous Computerized Adaptive Tests uses five-level scoring clinical item bank, maximum a posteriori estimation, maximum Fisher information amount selection and common fixed accuracy stopping rule to respectively manipulate the mean bias and variance of individualized prior. Simply moving the center of the unbiased prior to the true capability while maintaining the variance at 1 does not significantly shorten the CAT; the efficiency gain mainly comes from the smaller prior variance. However, a narrow and wrong prior may send the CAT into the low information area of ​​the item bank, resulting in a long test or the exhaustion of the item bank; it may also quickly reach the standard error threshold in the high information area, resulting in a hidden failure of "the question size is very short but the estimate is seriously biased".

    The second, more realistic simulation uses the global score given by the clinician to construct an individualized prior. Among 5,000 simulated examinees, the empirical prior shortened the number of questions for 68% of them, with a median reduction of 20% (1 question), but did not bring significant overall bias improvement. Enforcing a minimum number of questions or relaxing the prior can reduce risks, but it will also weaken the benefits of question volume. This article therefore directly supports us to separate the prior mean error, prior variance and the minimum number of questions, and report the bias, coverage rate, error early stopping and item bank exhaustion at the same time, instead of just comparing the average number of questions.

    What have you read in Chapter 7?¶

    Evaluating the Use of Prior Information to Individualize Start Item Selection is the study currently closest to "changing only the first item" in terms of mechanism. The author first used a simple regression of one completed quality of life domain to predict another domain in 10,084 cancer patient assessments; then only selected the first question of the target CAT at the predicted position, and restored the original process after the first question. When selecting the best single prediction field for each target field, the prediction-observation correlation ranges from 0.31 to 0.72, and 72% to 89% of the predictions are within 1 standard deviation of the observation score; if averaged across all prediction fields, the performance is significantly lower, and the best result in the summary cannot be generalized to any combination of fields.

    CAT simulation shows that the benefits of individualized first questions are mainly concentrated in questions 1 to 3 and in patients far away from the overall mean; the average reliability across domains in low partitions increases by 0.11, and most differences disappear after questions 4 to 5. Only the low physical function condition in the fixed-accuracy simulation had a maximum average reduction of 1.4 questions, and the remaining conditions had no more than 0.7 questions. Note also that the simulation uses a controlled starting point of “true score, true score plus or minus 5 or 10 points” and does not feed the person-by-person prediction errors from the empirical regression end-to-end into CAT. Therefore, it is a good demonstration of the local effect of first-question routing, but it does not prove that real auxiliary information can generally shorten the test significantly.

    What have you read in Chapter 8?¶

    Impact of Informed Starting Value on Longitudinal Computer Adaptive Tests in PROMIS Assessments simulates two longitudinal CATs using the official PROMIS adult and child item banks. The first time uses the standard \(N(0,1)\) prior; the second time sets the first final EAP to the prior mean and the prior standard deviation to 1, 0.75, 0.5, or 0.25. What is changed here is the complete prior instead of just the first question, and the value is the standard deviation: \(SD=0.5\) corresponds to variance 0.25, and \(SD=0.25\) corresponds to variance 0.0625.

    When the state changes are small, \(SD=0.5\) often gets the lowest RMSE with the same or less question volume, but the overall improvement is smaller and relies on item bank coverage. When the state changes are large, most adult fields are most accurate at \(SD=1\); the narrowest prior will strongly pull the second estimate back to the old score. Since \(SD=0.25\) is already smaller than the stopping threshold of \(SE<0.3\), the minimum four-question rule makes CAT almost all stop at the fourth question, forming a "shorter but less accurate" test. The most important methodological inspiration of the paper is that the longitudinal prior variance should include both the last scoring error and the true state change variance, and the accuracy of historical scores cannot be directly regarded as the uncertainty of the current state.

    What have you read in the first supplementary literature?¶

    Estimating Ability and Item-Selection Strategy in Self-Adapted Testing distinguishes two propositions that are often confused. Under the author's sequential model, the potential response of the unrepresented item satisfies missing at random; but if the difficulty selection strategy is related to ability, the strategy parameters and ability parameters are inseparable, and the selection mechanism must still enter the joint likelihood. Only when the two are independent can ability be estimated based solely on the IRT likelihood of actual responses.

    An example of 72 high school students completing a fixed 20-question English vocabulary SAT shows that examinee has different selection modes such as maintaining difficulty, adjusting with feedback, and tolerating failure, and the difficulty changes more frequently in the first half than in the second half. Fitting the front and rear halves separately reduces the AIC from about 5468 to 5228, which is against the assumption that the strategy remains unchanged throughout the game. The paper also found that the asymptotic standard error of the fixed item path is about 40% of the complete Bootstrap standard error, suggesting that the entire topic selection and stopping process must be repeated when verifying the self-assessment driven CAT. It did not examine the effects of question size shortening or individualized prior.

    What have you read in the second part of the supplementary literature?¶

    An Investigation of the Effects of Self-Adapted Testing on Examinee Effort and Performance in a Low-Stakes Achievement Test Compares fixed-item tests, CAT, question-by-question self-selected difficulty S-AT, and game-based S-AT with dynamic points gains and losses in a low-stakes college setting. The four groups were all fixed at 40 questions; test type had no significant impact on average ability, self-reported effort, and response time effort. The overall RTE was approximately .96, indicating that most responses were not judged as quick guesses by the 10-second threshold, but there was no evidence that the option further improved effort above this higher baseline.

    This study does not support the hypothesis that "low risk + self-selection will automatically increase motivation", nor does it examine the reduction in question size. When dealing with allocation at the session level, there are only 15 valid sessions, and the non-significant results cannot prove that the four forms are equivalent; the original table also has inconsistencies in the annotations of RTE sample size, SOS degree of freedom, and invigilator effect size. The research is more suitable to support a careful design: testing the routing value, measurement efficiency and motivational experience of self-evaluation signals separately, rather than treating the motivational benefits of the entire topic selection as an established mechanism.

    What have you read in the third supplementary literature?¶

    Understanding Test Takers' Choices in a Self-Adapted Test A three-state HMM was constructed for 583 adults on 40 choices each at seven levels of difficulty. The selection centers of low, medium, and high potential states approximately correspond to levels 1.33, 3.57, and 6.02, and the self-maintenance probabilities all exceed .90; this shows that small changes in observed levels are often just intra-state fluctuations, and the same person can actually switch states after feedback accumulation.

    The higher the pretest, the greater the probability of initially being in the medium or high state; after controlling for the pretest, the performance-goal group is more likely to start in the low state and less likely to leave the low state. When the cumulative accuracy rate is low, the number of transfers increases, and reliability is set to further adjust the transfer direction. However, the most eye-catching 66.1% to 73.8% increase probability in the paper comes from the extreme and sparse accuracy-reliability combination, which belongs to model extrapolation. AIC supports a 62-parameter model with complete goal interaction, while BIC chooses a simpler accuracy-setting reliability model, and cannot claim that complex interactions are unanimously supported by all criteria.

    This remains a retrospective process analysis of the complete sequence. It neither lets the HMM drive CAT nor calibrates its states as ability priors. The most direct implication for this project is to ablate a one-time self-assessment, the mean choice over the first \(k\) items, and online HMM filtering separately, while avoiding the use of current or future item outcomes to predict choices that have already occurred.

    What have you read in the first article of extended literature?¶

    Performing Automatic Exams proposes a two-stage automatic exam. In the pre-examination, students select the difficulty level one by one, randomly obtain items at that level, and receive correctness feedback; if they answer at least one question correctly, the formal examination starts from the highest difficulty level, otherwise it decreases one level from the lowest difficulty level selected. There are six fixed questions in the formal stage, and the level will be raised, lowered or maintained based on the score combination of the last two questions; if the remaining questions cannot reach 18/30 even if all the remaining questions are answered correctly, it will be terminated early.

    This is not modern IRT-CAT: the system has no underlying ability estimate, individualized prior, item information, or precision measures, and problems are directly assigned higher scores. The paper reporting system completed more than 300 exams and had 100 students take manual and automated exams; 90% of the scores were less than 3 points, but there was no common starting point comparison, complete consistency statistics, question accuracy comparison, or exposure analysis. It proves that there is historical precedent for "users participating in the startup and the algorithm then taking over", but it does not prove that this kind of startup can shorten CAT; the most important revelation for this item is that the pre-test questions must be included in the total burden, and the difficulty selection signal and the actual answer signal must be ablated separately.

    What have you read in the second extended article?¶

    a-Stratified Multistage Computerized Adaptive Testing illustrates that the information curve of high-discrimination questions is high and narrow: if the early ability estimate deviates from the true ability, the high-\(a\) questions matched by \(\widehat\theta\) may have almost no information at the true \(\theta\). Therefore, the author divides the item bank into four layers from low to high according to \(a\). Let the low \(a\) layer be used in the early stage of the test and the high \(a\) layer in the later stage. In the current layer, the two closest candidates are selected according to \(|b-\widehat\theta|\) and then randomly tested together.

    In three sets of fixed length simulations, STR significantly reduced exposure imbalance, the number of low-exposure questions, and test overlap. The overlap of the 40-question and 400-question item bank conditions dropped from 19% of FSH to 11%, which is close to the theoretical lower bound of 10%. However, in the 20-question condition of the real 3PLM parameters, the MSE of STR is .084, which is higher than FSH's .060. It cannot be generally claimed that there is no accuracy cost. When \(L,N\) is fixed, the average exposure rate is always \(L/N\), so the so-called "lower average exposure" in the abstract should not be taken as a conclusion; the method improves the exposure distribution rather than the conserved average value.

    There is no self-evaluation or individualized prior in this article, and it cannot guarantee maximum exposure for a single question. Its direct inspiration for this item is to treat \(b\) as individual position matching and \(a\) as uncertainty and item resource allocation issues: an accurate prior may allow high \(a\) questions to be used in advance, while an incorrect or fuzzy prior requires low \(a\) exploration. Subsequently, the fixed \(a\) layer and the posterior-variance-adaptive layer should be ablated and verified using the information at the real ability, Bias, RMSE, coverage rate, question volume and conditional exposure.

    What have you read in the third extended article?¶

    Differences Between Self-Adapted and Computerized Adaptive Tests synthesizes 19 ability comparisons and 8 posttest-anxiety comparisons from 15 studies. SAT ability estimates were, on average, \(d=.11\) higher than CAT estimates and .12 after reliability correction; posttest anxiety was, on average, \(d=.18\) lower and .19 after correction. Both effects are small. The higher ability estimates could reflect changes in anxiety or perceived control, or item-selection and scoring bias. The meta-analysis did not conduct a mediation analysis and therefore cannot claim that reduced anxiety caused better performance.

    This research synthesis also did not incorporate question size, standard error, time taken, or exposure. The Discussion paragraph states that fixed test length SAT is generally less accurate than CAT and cites one study of equivalent accuracy in which SAT took 43% longer, but this was not a pooled effect of the meta-analysis. Of the 19 ability effects, 13 were positive and 6 were negative; although all anxiety effects were positive, there were only 8. The research team is highly centralized, and the paper does not address effect dependence, heterogeneity, publication bias, or small sample \(d\) bias.

    The most important gap for this item is that the early evidence mainly compares the two endpoints of "algorithmic full process control" and "examinee full process control". There is no systematic inspection that only allows users to influence the first question or the first \(k\) question, and then the intermediate design taken over by individualized prior and CAT. Follow-up experiments should orthogonally separate the duration of control, prioritization into estimation, topic selection rules and feedback, and simultaneously measure anxiety, sense of control, question volume, accuracy and item bank exposure.

    Questions to be answered for subsequent reading¶

    1. Should self-reported information be mapped to a point estimate or a prior distribution with uncertainty?
    2. Which interface is more stable, let the examinee choose the first question, the previous question \(k\), or just report its own level?
    3. When a self-assessment error occurs, how many questions does a standard CAT require to correct the starting point of the error?
    4. Under the same stopping rule, how do the average question volume, tail question volume, bias, root mean squared error (root mean square error, RMSE) and coverage rate change?
    5. Do efficiency gains only occur in examinees at extreme levels of ability or symptoms?
    6. Will changes in the sense of autonomy, anxiety, and motivation change the answering process of the tested construct itself?
