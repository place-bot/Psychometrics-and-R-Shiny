    Frans et al (2023): Benefits and risks of empirical prior in clinical multilevel scoring CAT¶

    Key takeaway

    What was done: Frans et al. used two clinical multi-level scoring CAT simulations to manipulate the mean bias, variance and minimum number of questions of the empirical prior, respectively, and let the prior continuously affect MAP estimation, maximum information content selection and accuracy stop. What is gained: A correct and more accurate prior can shorten the test, but the gain in question volume mainly comes from the smaller variance; once the prior is narrow and wrong, it may not converge for a long time, or it may stop early with a small posterior standard error. Conclusions for this topic: Text or self-evaluation prior cannot be proven to be effective only by "reaching the SE threshold faster". It must also test the empirical error, coverage rate, item bank exhaustion and safety mechanism when the prior conflicts.

    Citation details¶

    Frans, N., Braeken, J., Veldkamp, B. P., & Paap, M. C. S. (2023). Empirical priors in polytomous computerized adaptive tests: Risks and rewards in clinical settings. Applied Psychological Measurement, 47(1), 48–63. DOI

    • Study type: Two fixed precision Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT) simulation study
    • Application scenario: clinical patient-reported outcome and structured interview of personality function
    • item model: five-level scoring graded response model (Graded Response Model, GRM)
    • ability estimate: maximum a posteriori estimation (Maximum A Posteriori, MAP)
    • Question selection rules: Select the question with the greatest Fisher information at the current MAP position
    • stopping rule: \(SE(\widehat\theta_p)\leq 0.316\), approximately corresponding to local reliability \(0.90\)
    • Core controls: prior meanbias and prior variance, and the minimum number of test questions

    Counterexamples that must be remembered in this topic

    Reaching the posterior standard error threshold only indicates that the model internally believes that the estimate is accurate, and does not guarantee that the estimate is close to the true capability. If an error prior is very narrow, CAT may only test a small number of items and stop; at this time, the posterior variance is small, but the posterior center may still be seriously wrong.

    1. Why this article is directly related to our research¶

    The previous documents respectively stated that auxiliary variables can construct an empirical prior, the empirical prior can continue to enter the question-by-question estimation, and the information generated by the examinee itself can also form a prior. Frans et al. go a step further and ask a more dangerous question:

    What happens to fixed-precision CAT if the individualized prior is not completely correct?

    This paper has four direct intersections with our approach:

    1. Prior affects both the current ability estimate and the actual topic selection path;
    2. Using the same precision stopping rule as the standard CAT, you can directly compare question volumes;
    3. Set the prior variance to \(1.00\), \(0.50\) and \(0.25\), which is very close to the design in which we intend to manipulate the prior strength;
    4. The system changes the bias of the prior mean relative to the true ability, directly demonstrating the risk when self-assessment is wrong.

    It does not study examinee self-evaluation, nor does it study only changing the first question. The "individualized prior" in the first study was constructed from the real \(\theta\) plus an artificially set bias, which is a controlled oracle simulation; the second study used the global score given by clinicians before the formal interview to simulate more realistic auxiliary information.

    2. Why does prior variance affect estimation, topic selection and stopping at the same time?¶

    For examinee \(p\), the individualized normal prior is written as:

    \[ \theta_p\sim N(\mu_p,\sigma_p^2). \]

    Among them:

    • \(\mu_p\) is the initial position given by the auxiliary information;
    • \(\sigma_p^2\) indicates the uncertainty of the information;
    • \(1/\sigma_p^2\) is the prior accuracy.

    Combined with the observed question-by-question responses \(\mathbf u_{p,1:t}\), the posterior is:

    \[ p(\theta_p\mid \mathbf u_{p,1:t}) \propto \left[ \prod_{s=1}^{t} P(U_{p i_s}=u_{p i_s}\mid\theta_p) \right] \phi(\theta_p;\mu_p,\sigma_p^2). \]

    The paper uses the posterior mode as the current ability estimate:

    \[ \widehat\theta_{p,t}^{\mathrm{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\theta} p(\theta\mid \mathbf u_{p,1:t}). \]

    Then select the remaining question with the greatest Fisher information at that position:

    \[ i_{t+1} = \arg\max_{i\in\mathcal R_t} I_i(\widehat\theta_{p,t}^{\mathrm{MAP}}). \]

    So even if the topic selection formula itself remains unchanged, changing prior will still change:

    \[ \text{Current MAP} \longrightarrow \text{Next question} \longrightarrow \text{new response evidence} \longrightarrow \text{Follow the entire path}. \]

    Why does the prior make CAT stop faster?¶

    Under the local approximation of a posteriori approximately normal:

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(\theta_p\mid \mathbf u_{p,1:t}) \approx \frac{1}{\sigma_p^{-2}+I_t(\theta_p)}, \]

    Among them, \(I_t\) is the accumulated information provided by the tested items. The narrower the prior and the larger \(\sigma_p^{-2}\), the less item information is required to reach the same posterior variance threshold.

    This also explains the risk: the stopping rule calculates the prior accuracy together with the item evidence. If the prior is accurate but the mean is wrong, the system may still stop with a small posterior standard error.

    Why didn't the paper set the prior variance smaller?

    The variance corresponding to the stopping threshold is approximately \(0.316^2=0.0999\). The author sets the minimum prior variance to \(0.25\), clearly stating that this is to prevent CAT from meeting the stopping condition before testing any item. If our simulation is to use \(\tau^2=0.10\), which falls almost exactly on the stopping boundary, we must explicitly specify that at least one question must be tested, or the stopping rule must be checked only after completing the first question.

    3. Simulation study one: Separate the prior bias and prior accuracy¶

    3.1 How to generate item bank¶

    Based on the empirical characteristics of the patient-reported outcome measurement information system (PROMIS) item bank, the author simulated respectively:

    \[ 100\text{a}N=30\text{item bank}, \qquad 100\text{a}N=60\text{item bank}. \]

    Each question has five response levels and is calibrated using GRM. The generation parameters are:

    • The fourth category threshold comes from the normal distribution of mean \(2.2\) and variance \(0.16\);
    • The stepwise distance to other thresholds comes from the logarithmic normal distribution of mean \(0.75\) and variance \(1.44\);
    • The discrimination comes from the truncated normal distribution of the position parameter \(3.5\) and the scale parameter \(1.0\), limited to \([1.5,5]\).

    The average item bank information is:

    \[ \theta\approx1.1 \]

    The highest place. This is in line with the characteristics of clinical item banks that mainly provide information in areas with higher pathological degrees.

    3.2 examinee and complete reaction data¶

    True capabilities are located at nine grid points:

    \[ \theta_p\in \{-1,-0.5,0,0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5,3\}. \]

    Each grid point simulates 100 people, so each item bank has:

    \[ 900\text{mock examinee}. \]

    The author first generates complete responses to all items in the item bank for each person, and then allows different prior conditions to select questions from the same set of potential responses. This within-examinee comparison reduces the interference of random response differences on conditional comparisons.

    3.3 Three types of priors¶

    Default standard normal prior¶

    \[ \theta_p\sim N(0,1). \]

    General clinical population a priori¶

    \[ \theta_p\sim N(1,1). \]

    Its center is closer to the position with the highest amount of information in the item bank.

    individualized prior¶

    \[ \theta_p\sim N(\mu_p,\sigma_p^2), \qquad \mu_p=\theta_p+\operatorname{bias}_0. \]

    Author orthogonal manipulation:

    \[ \operatorname{bias}_0 \in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}, \]

    and:

    \[ \sigma_p^2 \in\{1.00,0.50,0.25\}. \]

    This design separates two issues:

    • Whether the prior center is correct;
    • How confident the system is about this center.

    3.4 Common rules of CAT¶

    All conditions use the same:

    • First question selection: The amount of information is the largest at the prior mean;
    • Subsequent topic selection: The amount of information is the largest at the current MAP;
    • Stop standard: \(SE(\widehat\theta_p)\leq0.316\);
    • Minimum number of questions: Set to 1, 2, 3 or 4 questions respectively.

    Therefore, the difference in question volume under different conditions mainly comes from the impact of prior on the estimated path and cumulative accuracy, rather than different stopping thresholds.

    3.5 Evaluation indicators¶

    Author reports:

    1. Whether the target accuracy is achieved before the item bank is exhausted;
    2. Absolute question volume;
    3. Relative question volume:
    \[ \frac{n_p^{(\mathrm{personalized})}} {n_p^{(\mathrm{generic})}}; \]
    1. Estimate bias:
    \[ \operatorname{BIAS}(\widehat\theta_p) = \widehat\theta_p-\theta_p; \]
    1. The absolute bias difference between individualized prior and general prior.

    4. The result of simulation study one¶

    4.1 The depletion of item bank is concentrated in "extreme ability + error in the same direction prior"¶

    For the 60-question item bank, a CAT exceeding \(99.9\%\) reaches the target accuracy.

    For the 30-question item bank, the CAT of \(2.3\%\) still fails to reach the target after exhausting the item bank; among them, \(89.8\%\) appears in the individualized prior condition. Typical extreme situations are:

    \[ \theta_p=3, \qquad \operatorname{bias}_0=2, \qquad \mu_p=5. \]

    The item bank has almost no enough information around \(\theta=5\). The error prior sends the estimation and question selection to the end of the information in the item bank. CAT requires many questions to correct, or even uses up all the questions.

    4.2 Just moving the prior mean to the correct position does not significantly shorten the test¶

    The default average question size of \(N(0,1)\) prior is:

    • 60 item bank: 2.6 questions;
    • 30 item bank: 2.9 questions.

    The average number of questions for clinical \(N(1,1)\) prior is:

    • 60 item bank: 2.8 questions;
    • 30 item bank: Question 3.1.

    The most critical result in Figure 1 is: when the mean of the individualized prior is completely correct but the variance is still \(1\), compared with the standard normal prior, the number of questions does not decrease significantly.

    This means:

    "Start closer to true capability" is not the same thing as "fixed accuracy CAT stops earlier".

    The former mainly reduces the distance to the appropriate difficulty area; the latter also requires the prior itself to contribute enough accuracy.

    4.3 The real question volume income mainly comes from smaller prior variance¶

    When the prior mean is unbiased, the variance is reduced from \(1.00\) to \(0.25\), and the average number of questions is approximately shortened to the original:

    \[ \frac{1}{1.3}\approx0.77, \]

    That is approximately reduced by \(23\%\). The paper summarizes it in the discussion as a decrease in question volume of about \(30\%\).

    Two expressions should be distinguished here: the result part of the original text says "the shortening factor is 1.3"; strictly converted to the reduction relative to the original length, it is about \(1-1/1.3\approx23\%\). The Discussion section uses the looser "30% reduction" generalization.

    4.4 Short tests are not necessarily good news when the prior is wrong¶

    When the wrong prior pushes the starting estimate to the tail where the item bank has insufficient information:

    \[ \text{Less item information} \longrightarrow \text{Posterior accuracy increases slowly} \longrightarrow \text{The quiz becomes long or the item bank is exhausted}. \]

    But when the error prior happens to push the starting estimate into the information-rich region of the item bank:

    \[ \text{There is a lot of item information + the prior is very narrow} \longrightarrow \text{The stopping threshold is reached quickly} \longrightarrow \text{Not enough answers to correct the error prior}. \]

    So there will be a more subtle failure than "the test becomes longer": the number of questions is very short, but the final estimate is seriously biased. **

    The author also observed that the conditions in which the estimated bias is particularly large are often the conditions in which the question size is relatively short.

    4.5 Unbiased and biased prior estimation results¶

    The average estimated bias of the default standard normal prior is:

    \[ -0.09, \]

    The text of the paper writes the range of different ability points as \(-0.34\) to \(-0.13\), but this is arithmetically incompatible with the mean \(-0.09\); there is likely a symbol or typographical error in the second endpoint. The conclusion that can be safely retained is that the default prior will shrink the estimate towards the center 0, and the ability points away from 0 will be more biased; it is not appropriate to quote this range without checking the original result.

    The average bias of clinical \(N(1,1)\) prior is \(0.00\), and the different ability points are approximately between \(-0.23\) to \(0.22\).

    The individualized prior whose mean is equal to the true \(\theta\) is almost unbiased and has a range of approximately:

    \[ -0.03\text{to}0.02. \]

    However, once the prior mean is biased, the smaller the variance, the more strongly the final estimate is pulled towards the error center.

    5. Can the problem be solved with the minimum number of questions?¶

    5.1 It does reduce bias¶

    Table 1 compares no minimum number of questions to administering at least 2, 3, and 4 questions. The following individualized prior result aggregates only the unbiased condition and aggregates across three prior variances:

    Minimum number of questions Default \(N(0,1)\): average question size / absolute bias Clinical \(N(1,1)\): average number of questions / absolute bias Unbiased individualized prior: average number of questions / absolute bias
    1 2.61 / 0.132 2.84 / 0.112 2.26 / 0.012
    2 2.61 / 0.132 2.84 / 0.112 2.38 / 0.009
    3 3.26 / 0.104 3.45 / 0.090 3.21 / 0.004
    4 4.13 / 0.080 4.26 / 0.071 4.15 / 0.003

    As the minimum number of questions increases, the differences in question size and bias among the three priors decrease. When there are at least four questions, the question volume advantage of unbiased individualized prior basically disappears.

    5.2 It cannot completely rescue very narrow and seriously wrong priors¶

    Even if it is mandatory to administer at least four questions, Table 2 still shows:

    prior mean bias variance \(1.00\): absolute bias variance \(0.50\): absolute bias variance \(0.25\): absolute bias
    \(-2\) 0.111 0.216 0.403
    \(-1\) 0.051 0.100 0.188
    \(0\) 0.004 0.003 0.004
    \(1\) 0.054 0.102 0.190
    \(2\) 0.113 0.223 0.411

    Therefore, at least four questions are a safety valve, but not a complete fix for the wrong prior. Reducing prior accuracy also reduces risk, but also eliminates most of the question volume gains.

    Structural trade-offs revealed in paper

    The efficiency gains of an individualized prior and the harm of an incorrect prior both come from the same source: how much weight the system gives to auxiliary information. Making the prior wider and forcing more questions will increase robustness; but these safeguards will also weaken the effect of shortening the test.

    6. Simulation study two: more realistic clinical global score¶

    6.1 What is auxiliary information?¶

    The second study was based on a structured clinical interview measuring impairment in personality functioning. Before the formal interview, the clinician gave a global score of 0 to 4 through several screening questions to guide follow-up questions.

    Due to data protection restrictions, the authors did not directly use real patient responses, but conducted simulations based on relationships in existing clinical studies.

    6.2 item bank and examinee¶

    • item bank: 12 five-level scoring questions;
    • GRM discrimination range: \(2.03\) to \(3.03\), average \(2.47\);
    • Simulate examinee: \(N=5{,}000\);
    • Real ability: \(\theta_p\sim N(0,1)\);
    • The item bank is able to support target accuracy in the range of approximately \([-1.96,2.64]\).

    6.3 How to change global score into prior¶

    The author first simulates the global score according to the conditional frequency of the existing clinical sample based on the rounded integer value of the real \(\theta\), and then assigns a normal prior to each score:

    Global score Expected number of people prior mean \(\mu_p\) prior variance \(\sigma_p^2\)
    0 105 \(-2.4\) 0.640
    1 1,046 \(-1.0\) 0.359
    2 2,343 \(0.0\) 0.339
    3 1,464 \(0.9\) 0.597
    4 42 \(2.5\) 0.250

    Therefore, the global score determines both the position and the strength of the prior. Scores 1, 2, and 4 correspond to more precise priors; scores 0 and 3 correspond to wider priors.

    The only comparison conditions are:

    \[ N(0,1) \quad\text{vs.}\quad N(\mu_{g_p},\sigma_{g_p}^2). \]

    Both used the same MAP, maximum information content selection and fixed precision stopping rule; the second study did not set a minimum number of questions.

    7. The result of simulation study two¶

    7.1 Reach target accuracy¶

    In the default prior condition:

    • \(98\%\) reaches the target accuracy;
    • 104 people exhausted 12 questions and still did not reach the stopping criterion.

    In the empirical prior condition:

    • \(99\%\) reaches the target accuracy;
    • 56 people exhausted the item bank;
    • 50 of them also failed under the default prior condition;
    • The additional 6 people all belong to global score 0, the average real ability is \(-2.7\), and the range is \([-3.6,-1.8]\).

    The prior center corresponding to score 0 is \(-2.4\), which is close to or beyond the information support range of the low end of the item bank, so this group once again has the problem of "prior sending CAT to the low information area".

    7.2 Question volume¶

    Question sizes ranged from 3 to 12 questions for both conditions, with an overall median of 5 questions.

    Empirical prior conditions compared to the default prior:

    • The exame questions of \(68\%\) are shorter;
    • \(29\%\) has the same number of questions;
    • \(3\%\) has longer questions.

    Individual differences range from "doing 6 more questions" to "doing 8 fewer questions." The median number of questions decreases to:

    \[ 20\%=1\text{question}. \]

    The gains in question volume mainly occur in global scores 1, 2 and 4, where the prior is more accurate; scores 0 and 3 have smaller gains because of the wider prior. Another 5 examinees with a score of 0 were significantly longer under the empirical prior.

    7.3 It is estimated that bias has not improved overall¶

    Default prior conditions:

    \[ \operatorname{BIAS}\in[-1.10,1.22], \qquad \operatorname{median}=0.00, \qquad MAD=0.21. \]

    Empirical prior conditions:

    \[ \operatorname{BIAS}\in[-1.16,1.32], \qquad \operatorname{median}=-0.02, \qquad MAD=0.30. \]

    Among 5,000 people, the empirical prior produces a smaller absolute bias for the person \(51\%\), and the default prior is better for the other \(49\%\). The median absolute bias difference between the two conditions is 0.

    Therefore, the author's accurate conclusion is: empirical prior usually shortens the number of questions when the overall bias is approximately unchanged. It cannot be written as "empirical prior while generally improving accuracy".

    8. What do the two simulations together tell us?¶

    prior situation common paths Surface result real risk or benefit
    The mean is correct and the variance is large The topic selection position is better, but the accuracy provided by prior is limited The amount of questions may not be significantly shortened Safe but low return
    The mean is correct and the variance is small Quickly accumulates to stopping accuracy The amount of questions has been significantly shortened ideal income
    Mean error, pointing to low information area Select unmatched or low-information questions first The test is very long and the item bank may be exhausted. Easily detectable failures
    Mean error, pointing to high information area Prior and local items quickly narrow the posterior The quiz is short Hidden severe bias and premature stopping

    This table explains why you can’t just consider a “decrease in average question volume” as a method success. At least also watch:

    • Final bias and root mean squared error (root mean square error, RMSE);
    • coverage rate;
    • premature stopping rate under wrong prior conditions;
    • Item bank depletion rate and question volume increase;
    • Results layered by prior error direction and item bank information area.

    9. Boundary of evidence: This article proves nothing¶

    9.1 The individualized prior of the first study is an idealized construction¶

    \[ \mu_p=\theta_p+\operatorname{bias}_0 \]

    Construct prior directly using real capabilities. It is well suited to identifying mechanisms but does not demonstrate that self-assessment, contextual variables, or clinical judgment are of the same quality in the real world.

    9.2 The second study is still not an actual patient CAT experiment¶

    The relationship between global score and \(\theta\) comes from existing clinical data, but the answers, scores and CAT are all simulated. The paper cannot claim based on this that the number of questions will be reduced by 20% in the patient's actual test.

    9.3 It does not study examinee self-evaluation¶

    The source of information for the second study was the ratings given by clinicians before the formal interview, not the examinee's choice of difficulty or reporting of their own symptom ratings. It supports the methodology of "entering auxiliary information with errors into prior", but it is not behavioral evidence of self-evaluation.

    9.4 It only changes the first question without comparison.¶

    The empirical prior continues to feed into the MAP estimate and therefore continues to influence topic selection and final stopping at each step. It cannot answer: How will the returns and risks change if the prior only determines the first question and restores the standard normal estimate after the first question.

    9.5 Evaluation indicators are still incomplete¶

    The paper focuses on reporting the number of questions, estimated bias, and item bank exhaustion. There is no system report:

    • RMSE；
    • Interval coverage rate;
    • The frequency of “standard error is up to standard but the absolute error is very large”;
    • test overlap between item exposure and examinee;
    • Fairness among different groups.

    These are exactly the metrics that our experiments should complement.

    10. Direct implications for our “Self-Assessment into CAT” experiment¶

    10.1 Make prior position and prior strength independent factors¶

    You can’t just simulate “self-assessed accuracy.” A clearer ablation is:

    \[ \mu_p=\theta_p+\delta_p, \qquad \delta_p\in\{0,\pm0.5,\pm1,\pm2\}, \]

    and set independently:

    \[ \tau^2\in\{1,0.5,0.25,0.1\}. \]

    Only in this way can we answer: What are the consequences of the same self-evaluation error when the "confidence level" of the system is different.

    10.2 \(\tau^2=0.1\) requires special handling¶

    If the stopping condition is also:

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid\mathbf u) \leq0.1, \]

    Then the prior of \(\tau^2=0.1\) is close to reaching the standard when there is no answer yet. The experiment must specify:

    • Administer at least 1, 2 or 3 questions; or
    • The inspection is not allowed to stop until the first question; or
    • The stopping accuracy only calculates the incremental evidence provided by the item, instead of directly treating the prior variance as evidence of completing the measurement.

    The third type will change the meaning of the traditional Bayesian stopping rule and requires separate argumentation and cannot be implemented quietly.

    10.3 Make at least four basic conditions¶

    In order to distinguish the effects of "changing estimates" and "changing initial topic selection", four basic conditions can be retained:

    Conditions estimate prior First topic/early topic selection
    A standard CAT \(N(0,1)\) The maximum amount of information at the current estimate
    B Only individualized estimates \(N(\mu_s,\tau_s^2)\) The maximum amount of information at the current individualized posterior
    C Only self-evaluation routing \(N(0,1)\) First select questions in the \(b\) range corresponding to the self-assessment, and then return to the standard CAT
    D complete method \(N(\mu_s,\tau_s^2)\) Self-evaluation routing or control by uncertainty \(a\) layer

    Among them, B is the main method category that Frans et al. belongs to; C and D can identify whether our additionally designed item routing has independent contributions.

    10.4 Don’t just report the average number of questions¶

    It is recommended to report at least also:

    1. Average question volume, median question volume and 95% question volume quantile;
    2. bias, absolute bias, RMSE and coverage rate;
    3. The proportion of \(|\widehat\theta-\theta|\) that still exceeds the preset threshold when stopped;
    4. item bank depletion rate;
    5. The number of restored questions when prior answers conflict with earlier answers;
    6. Overall and item exposure grouped by real \(\theta\) and self-assessment grade;
    7. test overlap。

    10.5 Security mechanisms must also be gradually dismantled¶

    Can be added in sequence:

    1. Unprotected individualized prior;
    2. Minimum number of questions constraint;
    3. Expand prior variance when self-assessment is found to conflict with early responses;
    4. Prior tempering, so that the weight of auxiliary information gradually decays with the item evidence;
    5. Mixed priors, for example:
    \[ p_0(\theta\mid s) = \lambda N(\mu_s,\tau_s^2) + (1-\lambda)N(0,1). \]

    Frans et al. only tested the first two protection ideas, fixed minimum number of questions and uniformly relaxed priors. Dynamic conflict detection, robust hybrid prior, changing only the first question and exposure control are still parts that can be developed.

    11. Chinese paraphrases that can be quoted safely¶

    Support "empirical prior may shorten clinical CAT"¶

    Frans et al.'s (2023) clinical CAT simulation showed that under more realistic global-score conditions, the empirical prior enabled 68% of examinees to obtain a shorter test, with a 20% reduction in median question size (1 question), while the overall estimate bias was approximately equivalent to the standard normal prior condition.

    Support "prior accuracy is a common source of return and risk"¶

    Frans et al. (2023) found that the unbiased individualized prior did not significantly shorten the fixed precision CAT when the variance was still 1; the question volume benefit mainly appeared when the prior variance was smaller. However, the more precise the prior, the more serious the bias in the final estimate caused by its mean error.

    Support "The minimum number of questions is a costly safety valve"¶

    Enforcing a minimum number of questions can reduce the bias caused by wrong prior, but it will also reduce or even eliminate the question size advantage of individualized prior; therefore, robustness and efficiency cannot be regarded as two independent goals.

    Cannot be quoted like this¶

    • It cannot be written as "empirical prior can always shorten the test and improve accuracy at the same time";
    • It cannot be written as "examinee self-assessment has been proven to be safe to form clinical CAT prior";
    • The 20% decrease in the number of questions in simulation study 2 cannot be regarded as the result of a randomized trial with actual patients;
    • Reaching \(SE\leq0.316\) cannot be used to prove that the estimate must be accurate and unbiased.

    • Bock & Mislevy (1982): The classic basis for Bayesian EAP/MAP ability estimate in CAT.
    • van der Linden (1999): Empirically initialize potential ability estimates with auxiliary variables.
    • Matteucci & Veldkamp (2013): The efficiency gains of individualized empirical priors in fixed-accuracy CAT in educational contexts are the idealized baseline that this article directly extends and questions.
    • Reise & Waller (2009): Clinical scale item bank is usually small, item discrimination is high, and the background basis for centralized measurement of pathological intervals.
    • Babcock & Weiss (2012): A basis for fixed-precision CAT to set a minimum number of questions to prevent premature stopping.

    13. Read the conclusion carefully¶

    This paper advances the topic from "Is there any empirical prior benefit?" to "What risk does the benefit come at?" The most critical conclusion is not an average shortening percentage, but the following causal chain:

    \[ \text{narrower prior} \longrightarrow \text{Stronger shrinkage and faster posterior accuracy} \longrightarrow \begin{cases} \text{Prior correct: question volume income},\\ \text{Prior errors: bias, premature stop, or item bank exhausted}. \end{cases} \]

    Therefore, for our self-assessment CAT, the real methodological question is not "should we trust users", but: How to convert the reliability of self-assessment into appropriate prior variance, and allow the system to promptly withdraw this trust when early answers conflict with self-assessment. **
