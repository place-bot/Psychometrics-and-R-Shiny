## Question 1: Are these good items?

**Question 1 prompt / 第 1 题总题干**

> Read the following items. Based on what you have learned in this class, please explain if you think these items are good items. If not, please explain why and how would you improve them?

### 1(a) Tom Brady and Deflategate

**Assignment prompt / 作业题干**

> To you does it seem likely or unlikely that Tom Brady was not involved in the deflate gate?
>
> Likely / Unlikely

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    WK1 p. 6 lists **“Response Bias”** as one of five measurement problems. WK1 p. 7 says to determine the **“Target population,” “Latent construct(s),”** and **“Item type”** before writing an item. These phrases do not decide this item directly; they specify what must be checked: who answers, what is measured, and how the response is elicited.

**Course location**

- WK1 p. 6: measurement scales and response bias.
- WK1 p. 7: target population, cross-cultural use, and item type.
- WK2 p. 12: selected-response formats for attitude items.
- DeVellis & Thorpe, pp. 99--102: clarity, multiple negatives, and negatively worded items.

**Terminology in full**

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Application to this item |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 项目／题目 | item | The smallest measurement unit that presents a stimulus and elicits a scorable response | Here it includes the stem and the Likely/Unlikely options |
| 题干 | item stem | The text that specifies what the respondent must judge | “Was not involved” supplies the central negation |
| 否定措辞 | negative wording | Wording that expresses a proposition with not, never, unlikely, or an equivalent form | The stem contains not and the response scale contains unlikely |
| 复杂否定 | complex or multiple negation | A response task requiring two or more changes of logical direction | “Unlikely that ... was not involved” cannot be mapped by reading one word alone |
| 含糊性 | ambiguity | Wording that permits more than one reasonable interpretation | A rushed respondent may reverse whether Likely refers to involvement or noninvolvement |
| 二分反应 | binary response | A response format with exactly two outcomes | It cannot represent strength of uncertainty or distinguish neutrality from unfamiliarity |
| 筛选项 | filter or screening option | An option or prior item that determines whether a person has enough experience or knowledge to answer | “Not familiar enough to answer” prevents forced guessing |
| 构念无关难度 | construct-irrelevant difficulty | Difficulty introduced by language, memory, or background knowledge outside the target construct | Negation and Deflategate knowledge can contaminate the attitude score |
| 反应偏差 | response bias | A systematic response tendency not caused by the intended construct level | If some groups are more likely to reverse the negation, the error may be systematic |

The stem asks for a “likely or unlikely” judgment about someone who “was **not** involved.” A respondent must know the event, process the negation, and map the resulting judgment back to the two response labels. Errors caused by that extra cognitive burden do not represent the target attitude and therefore introduce construct-irrelevant error.

The binary options also discard attitude intensity. No option is available for respondents who do not know enough about Deflategate, forcing background knowledge into the attitude score.

**A clearer revision**

> How likely do you think it is that Tom Brady was involved in Deflategate?
>
> Very unlikely / Unlikely / Neither likely nor unlikely / Likely / Very likely / Not familiar enough to answer

This revision removes the negation, supplies an ordered intensity scale, and separates insufficient knowledge from a genuinely neutral judgment.

### 1(b) Compensation and benefits

**Assignment prompt / 作业题干**

> I am satisfied with my compensation and benefits package.
>
> Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neutral / Agree / Strongly Agree

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    The job-satisfaction example on WK2 p. 12 asks about one object: **“I feel I am being paid a fair amount for the work I do.”** It provides a useful contrast: one attitude response should correspond to one clear proposition.

**Course location**

- WK2 p. 12: Likert-type attitude items.
- WK2 pp. 19--20: “Are these good items?”
- DeVellis & Thorpe, p. 100: double-barreled items.

**Terminology in full**

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Application to this item |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 双重问题 | double-barreled item | One stem that asks about two claims that could receive different answers | A person may like compensation but dislike benefits |
| 潜在构念 | latent construct | An unobserved attribute inferred from a pattern of item responses | Job satisfaction should be represented by a set of defensible indicators |
| 单维性 | unidimensionality | The degree to which one dominant dimension explains the shared item variation | After splitting the item, evidence is still needed before combining the two scores |
| Likert 型项目 | Likert-type item | A single statement followed by ordered agreement categories | One item is Likert-type; a multi-item composite is commonly called a Likert scale |
| 中点 | neutral midpoint | The central response category denoting no directional position | Neutral should not be forced to represent opposite opinions about salary and benefits |
| 反应映射 | response mapping | The process of translating an internal judgment into an available response category | Two internal judgments cannot be mapped without loss to one button |
| 测量误差 | measurement error | The discrepancy between an observed score and the intended attribute | A forced compromise adds error unrelated to either true attitude |

The item asks about compensation and benefits at the same time. A respondent may be satisfied with salary but dissatisfied with benefits, so a single response cannot accurately express both judgments. The problem is not the Likert response format; it is the presence of two potentially separable claims in one stem.

**Revision principle: one principal claim per item**

> I am satisfied with my compensation.
>
> I am satisfied with my benefits package.

If the two items will later contribute to one scale score, item analysis should still test whether they behave as indicators of the same latent construct.

### 1(c) Frequency of corporal punishment

**Assignment prompt / 作业题干**

> How often do you use corporal punishment on your children at home?
>
> Never / Seldom / Occasionally / Sometimes / Often / Very Often / All the time

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    Under **“Social desirability,”** WK3 p. 14 proposes **“use randomized respondent scheme (RRS)”** as one response. WK1 p. 6 separately identifies **“Response Bias”** as a measurement problem.

**Course location**

- WK1 p. 6: measurement scales and response bias.
- WK3 p. 13: scale-use bias.
- WK3 p. 14: social desirability.
- WK3 pp. 15--18: randomized response schemes.
- DeVellis & Thorpe, pp. 106--107: vague response labels.
- DeVellis & Thorpe, pp. 117--118: item time frames.

**Terminology in full**

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Application to this item |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 敏感项目 | sensitive item | An item for which truthful disclosure may create stigma, punishment, shame, or privacy risk | Corporal punishment is normatively charged and vulnerable to underreporting |
| 社会赞许性偏差 | social-desirability bias | Systematic response distortion intended to present a socially approved image | Respondents may select a lower frequency than their true behavior |
| 模糊量词 | vague quantifier | A frequency or intensity word without a shared numerical boundary | Seldom, occasionally, and sometimes do not have stable ordering across people |
| 类别重叠 | overlapping categories | Two response categories that can both describe the same true state | The middle verbal labels have no mutually exclusive cutoffs |
| 互斥性 | mutual exclusivity | Every true response belongs to no more than one category | Numerical bins such as 0, 1, 2--3, and 4--5 reduce overlap |
| 穷尽性 | exhaustiveness | Every reasonable state has at least one available category | “I do not have a child” is needed for applicability |
| 参照期 | reference period or time frame | The explicit time window for recalling a behavior | “During the past 30 days” is more comparable than unrestricted “how often” |
| 行为具体化 | behavioral specificity | Replacing an abstract or evaluative label with an observable action | The item should state whether it means spanking, striking, or another act |
| 随机化反应方案 | randomized response scheme, RRS | A known random device hides individual truth while permitting group estimation | It can reduce systematic underreporting of a sensitive behavior |

The question concerns behavior constrained by strong social norms. Respondents may underreport it to present themselves favorably, creating systematic social-desirability bias. “Seldom,” “occasionally,” and “sometimes” have no shared numerical meanings and may overlap. The question also provides no time period, assumes that every respondent has a child, and assumes a common interpretation of “corporal punishment.”

**A behaviorally specific revision**

> During the past 30 days, how many times did you intentionally spank or strike your child as punishment?
>
> 0 / 1 / 2--3 / 4--5 / 6 or more / I do not have a child

The revision adds a time frame, an observable behavior, mutually exclusive quantity categories, and an applicability option. If truthful responding remains a serious concern, the RRS in Question 4 can exchange greater random variance for stronger individual privacy and potentially less social-desirability bias.

### 1(d) Lying with good intentions

**Assignment prompt / 作业题干**

> It is acceptable to lie as long as you have good intentions.
>
> Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neutral / Agree / Strongly Agree

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    WK2 p. 19 places **“All hand-guns should be licensed because ...”** in an “Are these good items” discussion. Its structure parallels this item: an attitude claim is followed by a reason or condition that may independently affect agreement.

**Course location**

- WK2 pp. 19--20: “Are these good items?”
- WK3 p. 14: social desirability.
- DeVellis & Thorpe, pp. 99--101: ambiguity, conditional wording, and double-barreled items.

**Terminology in full**

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Application to this item |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 条件性措辞 | conditional wording | A proposition restricted by if, unless, as long as, or a comparable clause | The response jointly depends on lying and on good intentions |
| 含糊性 | ambiguity | Key concepts lack sufficiently shared boundaries | Lie, acceptable, and good intentions can refer to very different situations |
| 评价性用语 | evaluative language | A word that carries a favorable or unfavorable value judgment | Good already frames the motive positively |
| 诱导性项目 | leading item | Wording that makes one response appear more reasonable or expected | Good intentions may provide a built-in justification for agreeing |
| 构念表征 | construct representation | The extent to which the item set covers the important components of a construct | One benevolent-lie scenario cannot represent general permissiveness toward lying |
| 内容抽样 | content sampling | Selecting a finite set of behaviors and contexts from a much larger construct domain | Multiple clear contexts are needed to avoid one example determining the score |
| 社会赞许性偏差 | social-desirability bias | Distortion driven by how the person wishes to be perceived | A respondent may disagree to appear honest or agree to appear compassionate |

“Acceptable,” “lie,” and “good intentions” may evoke very different situations across respondents. The phrase “as long as” also binds a judgment about lying to a judgment about motivation. A white lie, temporary withholding of medical information, academic dishonesty, and a lie intended to protect someone are not psychologically equivalent contexts.

**A more specific revision**

> It is acceptable to withhold distressing information temporarily when doing so is intended to protect another person.

Even this item samples only one behavioral context. A measure of general lying permissiveness should contain several clearly worded items covering theoretically relevant contexts, followed by an evaluation of their internal structure.

>
> B. eskimo curlew
>
> C. pigeon
>

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    WK2 p. 10 emphasizes **“Selecting an appropriate item format ... for the intended population.”** WK3 p. 1 lists **“Correct/Incorrect”** and **“Formula scoring”** as scoring choices for optimal-performance items. Both assume that the key can be defended clearly.

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Application to this item |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 最佳表现测验 | optimal-performance test | A test asking respondents to demonstrate their highest knowledge or ability under a correctness standard | A biology achievement item is optimal-performance rather than self-report |
| 选择题 | multiple-choice item | A selected-response item with a stem, one or more keys, and distractors | This item expects one best answer |

The answer to a knowledge-test item must be defensible and unambiguous. The original alternatives mix broad animal categories with more specific species names. Broad categories may include both living and extinct members, while the status of an obscure species may itself invite disagreement. An incorrect response can therefore reflect an unclear item boundary rather than deficient biology knowledge.

> Which of the following species is extinct?
>
> A. Dodo
>
> B. Chimpanzee
>
> C. Bald eagle
>
> D. Blue whale

The revised alternatives are at similar taxonomic levels, one option is clearly keyed, and the remaining options are plausible but clearly incorrect distractors.

### 1(f) The U.S.-coin arithmetic item

**Assignment prompt / 作业题干**

> You have a quarter, three pennies, two dimes, and four nickels. How much money in total do you have in cents?

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    WK2 p. 4 describes fairness as working to **“minimize barriers to valid score interpretations”** and using accommodations to **“remove construct-irrelevant barriers.”** WK1 p. 7 also says to consider the **“Target population”** and **“Cross cultural usage.”**

**Course location**

- WK1 p. 7: target population and cross-cultural use.
- WK2 p. 4: fairness and construct-irrelevant barriers.
- WK2 p. 10: an item format must be feasible for the intended population.

**Terminology in full**

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Application to this item |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 公平性 | fairness | Designing, administering, and interpreting scores so they reflect the intended construct rather than irrelevant barriers | Equal arithmetic ability should not yield different scores solely because coin names are unfamiliar |
| 目标群体 | target population | The population to which the intended score interpretation will generalize | The demand may be appropriate for U.S. pupils taught coins but not for an international sample |
| 文化负载 | cultural loading | Dependence of performance on culture-specific experiences, institutions, or common knowledge | Quarter, dime, and nickel require U.S. cultural and language knowledge |
| 语言需求 | linguistic demand | Vocabulary, syntax, and reading required to understand an item | Coin vocabulary is an additional demand if the target is only addition |
| 构念无关方差 | construct-irrelevant variance | Score variation produced by factors excluded from the construct definition | Unfamiliarity with coin values contaminates an arithmetic score |
| 调整措施 | accommodation | Removal of an irrelevant barrier without changing the target construct | Coin images or printed values are appropriate only if coin identification is not part of the construct |
| 测试蓝图 | test blueprint | A plan specifying content, cognitive demand, item format, and weights | It must state whether the item measures addition, currency knowledge, or both |
| 预期分数用途 | intended score use | The interpretation or decision the score is designed to support | A classroom exercise and a national high-stakes test require different fairness evidence |

If the target construct is integer addition, the item also requires knowledge of the names and values of U.S. coins. For students unfamiliar with U.S. currency or English coin vocabulary, an error may reflect cultural knowledge rather than arithmetic skill. The resulting score is contaminated by construct-irrelevant variance.

**The appropriate revision depends on the testing purpose**

- If the construct is arithmetic, ask for (25+3+20+20) directly.
- If practical currency use is part of the construct, retain the coins but state that currency identification belongs in the test blueprint and verify that the target population received relevant instruction.
- If language barriers should be minimized, show coin images and values rather than names alone.

## Question 2: An analytic scoring rubric

**Assignment prompt / 作业题干**

> A department asks faculty in each area to evaluate their graduate students and provide informative feedback to them. Our department does not do this, but suppose the department asks faculty to place each student in three performance categories. Please come up with a scoring rubric following the analytical approach.

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    WK3 p. 1 says that scoring **“is a separate facet of item/scale development.”** WK3 p. 11 explains the core halo-effect mechanism: component judgments can **“be influenced by one's overall impression.”** A rubric therefore needs more than three labels; it must separate the overall impression into observable dimensions.

### 2.1 Analytic versus holistic scoring

**Course location**

- WK1 p. 7: establish the purpose, target population, and latent construct first.
- WK1 p. 10: define the construct domain, blueprint, review, and pilot process.
- WK1 pp. 13--14: content coverage, task analysis, and subject-matter experts.
- WK3 p. 1: scoring is a distinct facet of item and scale development.
- WK3 p. 11: the halo effect.

Holistic scoring asks a rater to form one overall impression. Analytic scoring decomposes performance into explicit dimensions, scores each dimension, and combines the ratings using a prespecified rule. The analytic approach makes the evidence for a decision more transparent and can reduce halo effects: a favorable overall impression should not automatically elevate every component rating.

### 2.2 Terminology in full

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Operationalization in this task |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 评分量规 | scoring rubric | A scoring tool that prespecifies criteria, performance levels, anchors, and aggregation rules | Faculty use one shared rubric instead of private impressions |
| 评价维度 | criterion or dimension | A distinct aspect of performance evaluated separately | Research, methodology, and professional performance can be three dimensions |
| 表现等级 | performance level | An ordered quality category within a criterion | Needs improvement, Meets, and Exceeds form three levels |
| 分析式评分 | analytic scoring | Rating separate dimensions and then combining them by rule | It supplies diagnostic feedback about specific strengths and needs |
| 整体式评分 | holistic scoring | Assigning one overall score from a global impression | It is faster but gives little evidence for why a category was assigned |
| 行为锚点 | behavioral anchor | An observable behavior that defines what a level means | “Submits reproducible analyses on time” is more scorable than “very good” |
| 光环效应 | halo effect or halo error | A systematic influence of a global impression on multiple component judgments | Perceived intelligence may inflate ratings of rigor, teamwork, and reliability |
| 评分者间信度 | inter-rater reliability | The consistency of scores assigned by different raters to the same performance | Multiple faculty should give similar criterion ratings to the same case |
| 评分者校准 | rater calibration | Practice and feedback using shared examples to align interpretation of the rubric | Faculty discuss benchmark and boundary cases before operational scoring |
| 内容效度 | content validity | Adequacy with which rubric criteria represent the intended training outcomes | Dimensions should derive from program outcomes and faculty SMEs |
| 证据记录 | evidence record | Concrete work products or observations supporting each rating | A rating cites a paper, analysis, presentation, or collaboration record |
| 补偿式规则 | compensatory rule | Strength on one criterion can offset weakness on another | A simple 3--9 sum is compensatory |
| 合取式规则 | conjunctive rule | Every critical criterion must meet a minimum level | Ethics may require a minimum regardless of strengths elsewhere |

### 2.3 A three-level analytic rubric

| Criterion | 1 — Needs improvement | 2 — Meets expectations | 3 — Exceeds expectations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Research and conceptual competence | Demonstrates important gaps in theory or literature; the research question is unclear or disconnected from evidence | Accurately explains major theory and formulates a feasible question | Integrates multiple literatures, identifies a meaningful gap, and formulates a valuable new question |
| Methodological competence | Makes consequential design or analysis errors and requires extensive guidance | Selects and implements generally appropriate methods; major claims are supported | Independently justifies methods, checks assumptions, and conducts robustness analyses |
| Professional performance | Frequently misses deadlines; communication, collaboration, or research records are inadequate | Works reliably and on time, communicates clearly, and follows research norms | Proactively advances the work, demonstrates strong independence, and supports the team effectively |

Sum the three ratings to obtain a total from 3 to 9, then map it to three final categories:

- 3--4: Needs improvement
- 5--7: Meets expectations
- 8--9: Exceeds expectations

### 2.4 What else does a defensible rubric require?

- **Behavioral anchors:** Define levels using observable behavior rather than circular labels such as “good” or “average.”
- **Content validity:** Dimensions and weights should follow program goals and faculty subject-matter expertise, not an individual rater's momentary preference.
- **Inter-rater reliability:** Multiple faculty members should practice on common examples and evaluate whether their ratings agree sufficiently.
- **Evidence record:** Each component rating should be supported by concrete evidence.
- **Decision rule:** Category thresholds, treatment of missing evidence, and whether a critical weakness can be compensated by strength elsewhere should be specified in advance.

!!! warning "Three labels are not automatically an analytic rubric"
    A scale containing only “poor / average / good” is still an overall impression. Analytic scoring requires **multiple construct-relevant criteria + behavioral anchors for every level + an explicit aggregation rule**.

## Question 3: Four scoring algorithms for a Mark-M item

**Assignment prompt / 作业题干**

> A Mark-M item has 10 answer options. What are the following are the capital of a country?
>
> Rio de Janeiro / New York / Santiago / Shanghai / Cape Town / Prague / Berlin / Dubai / Cairo / Barcelona
>
> **(a)** What is your answer to this question? (This does not count towards your grade.)
>
> **(b)** Come up with four scoring algorithms and explain their advantages and disadvantages.
>
> **(c)** The correct answer is Santiago, Prague, Berlin and Cairo. All the other answers are incorrect (at least for now!). Use the four scoring mechanisms to score yourself given your answer in (a).

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    WK2 p. 15 calls the format **“Pick K out of N; or multiple-mark items”** and notes that K is sometimes given and sometimes omitted. The WK3 p. 2 table is titled **“Different Scoring Algorithms for Mark-M Items”** and lists SA1--SA8. The assignment asks for four, not for applying all eight.

### 3.1 Item format and notation

**Course location**

- WK2 p. 15: Pick \(K\) out of \(N\) and multiple-mark items.
- WK3 p. 1: Mark-M items, partial credit, and scoring choices.
- WK3 p. 2: eight Mark-M scoring algorithms; the assignment asks the student to select and explain four.

The item contains 10 cities. Santiago, Prague, Berlin, and Cairo are the four keyed correct options. Define:

| Symbol | Slide wording | Full meaning | Value or relation in this item |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| \(S\) | respondent's score | The final item score; the slide table sets its maximum to 1 | Depends on the selected algorithm |
| \(TO\) | total number of item options | The number of all presented alternatives | \(TO=10\) |
| \(CO\) | number of keyed correct options | The number of alternatives identified as correct in the scoring key | \(CO=4\) |
| \(MCO=m\) | correct options marked | Correct alternatives selected by the respondent, or hits | \(0\leq m\leq4\) |
| \(MIO=i\) | incorrect options marked | Distractors selected by the respondent, or false positives | \(0\leq i\leq6\) |
| \(CC\) | correct choices | All correct binary decisions: marked keys plus unmarked distractors | \(CC=m+(6-i)\) |
| \(IC\) | incorrect choices | All incorrect binary decisions: marked distractors plus omitted keys | \(IC=i+(4-m)\) |
| \(MO\) | marked options | Every marked alternative, correct or incorrect | \(MO=m+i\) |
| \(x\) | penalty | A penalty used by some rules for excessive marking | Used only by the relevant scoring rule |

An option-level “choice” is the binary decision to mark or not mark each option. Correctly leaving a distractor unmarked is therefore a **correct rejection** and contributes to \(CC\).

### 3.2 Outcome types and terminology

| True option status × response | 中文 | English | Meaning in this item |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Correct and marked | 命中 | hit or true positive | A capital that should be selected is selected |
| Correct and unmarked | 漏选 | miss or false negative | A required capital is omitted |
| Incorrect and marked | 误选／假阳性 | false positive | A noncapital is incorrectly selected |
| Incorrect and unmarked | 正确拒绝 | correct rejection or true negative | A distractor is correctly left blank |

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 多重作答题 | multiple-mark item | An item permitting or requiring more than one marked option | The response is an option vector rather than one A/B/C/D choice |
| 从 N 选 K | Pick \(K\) out of \(N\) | Select \(K\) options from \(N\), with \(K\) either disclosed or undisclosed | Disclosing \(K=4\) changes guessing strategy and difficulty |
| 选择反应 | selected response | A response chosen from alternatives supplied by the test | Scoring depends on option design and the scoring key |
| 二分计分 | dichotomous scoring | An item score with only two values, usually 0 and 1 | Exact match is dichotomous |
| 部分计分 | partial-credit scoring | Intermediate credit for a response that is not completely correct | It preserves information from knowing three answers but missing one |
| 公式计分 | formula scoring | A formula combining hits, omissions, and false selections | Each formula operationally defines a different meaning of mastery |
| 正确命中率 | hit rate or sensitivity | The proportion of all keyed options that are selected | \(MCO/CO\) measures detection of correct alternatives |
| 假阳性率 | false-positive rate | The proportion of all distractors that are selected | \(MIO/(TO-CO)\) captures overmarking or indiscriminate selection |
| 反应策略 | response strategy | A selection rule adopted to maximize expected score | A severe penalty can make knowledgeable respondents reluctant to select |
| 分数边界 | score bounds | The minimum and maximum values an algorithm permits | SA2 and SA4 may be negative unless scores are truncated |

### 3.3 Four algorithms and their measurement meanings

| Algorithm | Definition | What it rewards or penalizes | Advantage | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SA1 Exact match | \(S=1\) if \(IC=0\); otherwise \(S=0\) | Credit only for a completely correct response pattern | Simple, strict, and resistant to partial-credit strategies | Omitting one correct answer produces zero; little information is retained |
| SA2 Correct minus incorrect | \(S=(CC-IC)/TO\) | Rewards correct decisions and symmetrically penalizes incorrect decisions | Distinguishes partial knowledge from total failure | Negative scores are possible; strategic behavior can be complex with unequal key proportions |
| SA3 Proportion of correct decisions | \(S=CC/TO\) | Weights every option-level decision equally | Intuitive and bounded between 0 and 1 | When distractors are numerous, leaving everything unmarked can earn a high score |
| SA4 Hit rate minus false-positive rate | \(S=MCO/CO-MIO/(TO-CO)\) | Balances correct detection against false selection | Prevents the larger distractor set from dominating the score | May be negative and is less immediately intuitive than a percentage |

SA4 has the structure of sensitivity minus false-positive rate:

\[
S_{SA4}
=
\frac{\text{correct options selected}}{\text{all correct options}}
-
\frac{\text{incorrect options selected}}{\text{all incorrect options}}.
\]

It distinguishes the ability to identify correct options from the tendency to falsely identify distractors as correct.

!!! note "Why does the slide list eight algorithms?"
    WK3 p. 2 also lists SA5--SA8, showing that a Mark-M response has no single inevitable score. Some rules introduce chance penalties, some mainly reward hits, and some use half-credit thresholds. The embedded source table is cropped on the right and in its footnote and supplies no complete citation for the original study; this guide does not invent the ambiguous notation. The four clearly legible SA1--SA4 rules satisfy the assignment.

### 3.4 Apply all four algorithms to one's own response

Substituting \(TO=10\) and \(CO=4\):

\[
S_{SA1}=
\begin{cases}
1,&m=4\text{ and }i=0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

\[
S_{SA2}=\frac{m-i+1}{5},
\qquad
S_{SA3}=\frac{m+6-i}{10},
\qquad
S_{SA4}=\frac{m}{4}-\frac{i}{6}.
\]

For example, suppose a respondent marks three correct cities, omits Cairo, and marks no distractor. Then \(m=3,i=0\):

| Algorithm | Score |
| --- | ---: |
| SA1 | \(0\) |
| SA2 | \(0.80\) |
| SA3 | \(0.90\) |
| SA4 | \(0.75\) |

The different scores show that a scoring rule is not merely a technical afterthought. It operationally defines which response pattern counts as mastery.

## Question 4: Randomized response scheme

**Assignment prompt / 作业题干**

> An educational psychologist interested in investigating cheating behavior among high school students tries to get around social desirability by using the following scheme.
>
> “Question: Have you ever cheated on any assignment or exam? Please flip a coin without showing it to anyone else. If you get a head, please answer truthfully. If you get tail, please answer untruthfully. Since I don't know whether you get a head or tail from the flip, I'll never know whether your answer is truthful or not; therefore, you do not need to worry about being identified as having cheated or not. Please circle your answer: Yes / No.”
>
> **(a)** She collected data from 120 students. And she gave them loaded coins, with probability of head of .7, and probability of tail of .3. In the end she found that 80 students responded “yes” and 40 responded “no” to her question. Please estimate the percentage of students ever cheated on an assignment or exam.
>
> **(b)** What if she collected data from 12 students, and 8 responded “yes”? What is your estimate of percentage of students ever cheated? Are you more confident in your estimate in a) or in b)? Why?
>
> **(c)** Consider asking the question directly to the participants. Suppose out of the 120 participants, 80 students responded “yes” and 40 responded “no”. What is your estimate of the percentage of students ever cheated on an assignment or exam? Compared to the answer in a), is it lower or higher or the same?
>
> **(d)** By using the RRS in a) you are supposed to get less biased estimate but your estimate has larger variability. In order to reach the same level of precision in your estimate as in c), how big a sample size you need at the minimum under the RRS in a)? Required for quant grad student only. Optional for others.

### 4.1 Why introduce randomization?

!!! quote "Slide wording / 课件原话"
    WK3 p. 10 describes measurement error as **“Random fluctuation”** that **“In the long run cancels itself out,”** and systematic bias as **“Non-random”** behavior that **“In the longrun accumulates.”** Under social desirability, WK3 p. 14 proposes **“use randomized respondent scheme (RRS).”**

**Course location**

- WK3 p. 10: measurement error versus systematic bias.
- WK3 p. 14: social desirability.
- WK3 p. 15: the loaded-coin RRS example.
- WK3 pp. 16--17: the estimator and worked example.
- WK3 p. 18: failure with a fair coin, a small sample, or an individual-level objective.

Direct questions about cheating, bullying, or other sensitive behaviors may lead to systematic underreporting. A randomized response scheme prevents the researcher from knowing whether any particular response reveals the individual's true status, while the known randomization probability still permits estimation of the population prevalence.

The design trades two sources of error:

- **Systematic bias** pushes estimates in a direction and does not cancel through repetition.
- **Random measurement or sampling error** fluctuates across samples and can cancel on average, but it reduces precision.

RRS deliberately accepts additional random variance in exchange for privacy and potentially less social-desirability bias.

### 4.2 Probability model and sampling distribution

| 中文 | English term | Definition | Role in this problem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 敏感特征 | sensitive trait | A true status that a respondent may hide because of stigma, punishment, or privacy risk | Ever cheating is a binary status that may not be reported candidly |
| 总体比例 | prevalence or population proportion | The proportion of the population whose true status is Yes | The target parameter is \(\pi\), not the observed sample Yes proportion |
| 随机化反应方案 | randomized response scheme, RRS | A known random device hides individual truth while allowing population estimation | The coin determines whether to tell the truth or reverse it |
| 负载硬币 | loaded coin | A coin or equivalent device whose two outcome probabilities are unequal | \(p=.7\) preserves some privacy while allowing \(\pi\) to be identified |
| 潜在真实状态 | latent true status | The person's actual sensitive status, which the researcher cannot observe directly | The student's true cheating status |
| 观察反应 | observed response | The Yes/No response visible after the randomization rule | Its Yes probability is \(\lambda\), generally not \(\pi\) |
| Bernoulli 分布 | Bernoulli distribution | The distribution of one binary variable with success probability \(\lambda\) | Each observed Yes indicator \(X_j\) is Bernoulli |
| 二项分布 | binomial distribution | The distribution of the sum of \(n\) independent identically distributed Bernoulli variables | \(Y\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,\lambda)\) counts observed Yes responses |
| 参数 | parameter | A fixed but unknown quantity describing a population | \(\pi\) and the mechanism-implied \(\lambda\) |
| 统计量 | statistic | A random quantity calculated from sample data | \(Y\) and \(\hat\lambda=Y/n\) |
| 估计量 | estimator | A rule mapping sample statistics to an estimate of a parameter | \(\hat\pi=(\hat\lambda+p-1)/(2p-1)\) |
| 偏差 | bias | The difference between an estimator's long-run mean and its target | Direct questioning can be downward biased through concealment |
| 方差 | variance | The squared scale of an estimator's fluctuation across repeated samples | The randomization device increases variance |
| 标准误 | standard error, SE | The standard deviation of an estimator's sampling distribution | It compares the stability of the 120-person and 12-person estimates |
| 精度 | precision | The inverse idea of random variability; smaller variance or SE means greater precision | Part (d) raises RRS precision to the direct-questioning level |
| 可识别性 | identifiability | Whether the observed distribution uniquely determines the target parameter | At \(p=.5\), every \(\pi\) gives \(\lambda=.5\), so \(\pi\) is not identifiable |
| plug-in 估计 | plug-in estimate | Replacing an unknown probability in a variance formula with its sample estimate | Part (d) uses \(\hat\lambda=2/3\) to solve for \(n\) |

Let:

- \(\pi\) be the population prevalence of having cheated;
- \(p=0.7\) be the probability of heads, under which the respondent answers truthfully;
- \(1-p=0.3\) be the probability of tails, under which the respondent answers untruthfully;
- \(\lambda=P(\text{observed Yes})\).

An observed Yes occurs through one of two mutually exclusive paths: a person who has cheated gets heads and answers truthfully, or a person who has not cheated gets tails and reverses the truthful answer. Therefore,

\[
\lambda=p\pi+(1-p)(1-\pi).
\]

Each observed response \(X_j\) follows a Bernoulli distribution, and the total number of Yes responses \(Y\) follows a binomial distribution:

\[
X_j\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\lambda),
\qquad
Y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_j\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,\lambda).
\]

Solving the first equation for \(\pi\) gives

\[
\hat\pi
=
\frac{\hat\lambda+p-1}{2p-1},
\qquad
\hat\lambda=\frac Yn.
\]

The denominator \(2p-1\) explains why this version fails with a fair coin. When \(p=0.5\), the denominator is zero and the observed Yes probability no longer identifies \(\pi\).

### 4(a) Eighty Yes responses among 120 students

\[
\hat\lambda=\frac{80}{120}=\frac23.
\]

\[
\hat\pi
=
\frac{\frac23+0.7-1}{2(0.7)-1}
=
\frac{0.3667}{0.4}
\approx0.9167.
\]

The estimated prevalence of ever having cheated is approximately \(91.67\%\). The observed proportion \(80/120\) is not itself the target prevalence: it combines truthful responses with forced reversals.

### 4(b) Eight Yes responses among 12 students

Because \(8/12=2/3\), the point estimate remains \(91.67\%\). The sample, however, is only one tenth as large and the estimate is much less stable.

\[
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\pi)
=
\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{n(2p-1)^2},
\]

\[
SE(\hat\pi)
=
\sqrt{\frac{\hat\lambda(1-\hat\lambda)}{n(2p-1)^2}}.
\]

With the same observed proportion, reducing \(n\) from 120 to 12 multiplies the standard error by \(\sqrt{10}\). The estimate in part (a) is therefore more precise.

### 4(c) Direct questioning

Under direct questioning, an observed Yes is treated as a truthful Yes:

\[
\hat\pi_{\text{direct}}=\frac{80}{120}=0.6667.
\]

This is lower than the RRS estimate of \(0.9167\). The comparison does not prove that the RRS value is the truth. The conceptual point is that direct questioning has less sampling variance but may suffer from social-desirability bias, whereas RRS increases variance while protecting individual status and attempting to reduce systematic underreporting.

### 4(d) Sample size for equal precision

The plug-in variance of the direct estimator is

\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\pi_D)
=
\frac{(2/3)(1/3)}{120}
=
\frac{1}{540}.
\]

If a future RRS sample uses \(\hat\lambda=2/3\), then

\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\pi_R)
=
\frac{(2/3)(1/3)}{n(0.4)^2}
=
\frac{25}{18n}.
\]

Requiring the RRS variance to be no larger than the direct-question variance yields

\[
\frac{25}{18n}\leq\frac{1}{540}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
n\geq750.
\]

Thus, under the plug-in comparison based on the observed proportions in the question, at least \(750\) participants are required.

!!! note "The sample-size answer depends on the comparison assumption"
    The value \(750\) uses the observed \(2/3\) in each design as its plug-in probability. If the comparison instead holds a common true prevalence \(\pi=2/3\) across designs, the RRS observation probability is \(\lambda=0.3+0.4(2/3)=17/30\), producing a minimum of \(829\) after rounding up. A complete response should state the assumption; \(750\) is the most direct answer under the proportions supplied in the assignment.

### 4.3 When should this version not be used?

- \(p=0.5\): the prevalence \(\pi\) is not identifiable.
- The sample is small: randomization makes the estimator unstable and an unconstrained estimate may even fall outside \([0,1]\).
- The objective is an individual decision: RRS intentionally hides individual status and is designed for group prevalence.
- Respondents misunderstand or fail to follow the randomization instructions: the assumed response mechanism is broken and the estimator can be biased.
