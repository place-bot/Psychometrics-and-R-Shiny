## Question 1: Are these good items?

### 1(a) Tom Brady and Deflategate

> To you does it seem likely or unlikely that Tom Brady was not involved in the deflate gate?

**Course location**

- WK1 p. 6: measurement scales and response bias.
- WK1 p. 7: target population, cross-cultural use, and item type.
- WK2 p. 12: selected-response formats for attitude items.
- DeVellis & Thorpe, pp. 99--102: clarity, multiple negatives, and negatively worded items.

**Key terms**

- negative wording
- multiple negatives
- binary response
- ambiguity
- construct-irrelevant difficulty

The stem asks for a “likely or unlikely” judgment about someone who “was **not** involved.” A respondent must know the event, process the negation, and map the resulting judgment back to the two response labels. Errors caused by that extra cognitive burden do not represent the target attitude and therefore introduce construct-irrelevant error.

The binary options also discard attitude intensity. No option is available for respondents who do not know enough about Deflategate, forcing background knowledge into the attitude score.

**A clearer revision**

> How likely do you think it is that Tom Brady was involved in Deflategate?
>
> Very unlikely / Unlikely / Neither likely nor unlikely / Likely / Very likely / Not familiar enough to answer

This revision removes the negation, supplies an ordered intensity scale, and separates insufficient knowledge from a genuinely neutral judgment.

### 1(b) Compensation and benefits

> I am satisfied with my compensation and benefits package.

**Course location**

- WK2 p. 12: Likert-type attitude items.
- WK2 pp. 19--20: “Are these good items?”
- DeVellis & Thorpe, p. 100: double-barreled items.

**Key terms**

- double-barreled item
- unidimensionality
- Likert-type response

The item asks about compensation and benefits at the same time. A respondent may be satisfied with salary but dissatisfied with benefits, so a single response cannot accurately express both judgments. The problem is not the Likert response format; it is the presence of two potentially separable claims in one stem.

**Revision principle: one principal claim per item**

> I am satisfied with my compensation.
>
> I am satisfied with my benefits package.

If the two items will later contribute to one scale score, item analysis should still test whether they behave as indicators of the same latent construct.

### 1(c) Frequency of corporal punishment

> How often do you use corporal punishment on your children at home?
>
> Never / Seldom / Occasionally / Sometimes / Often / Very Often / All the time

**Course location**

- WK1 p. 6: measurement scales and response bias.
- WK3 p. 13: scale-use bias.
- WK3 p. 14: social desirability.
- WK3 pp. 15--18: randomized response schemes.
- DeVellis & Thorpe, pp. 106--107: vague response labels.
- DeVellis & Thorpe, pp. 117--118: item time frames.

**Key terms**

- social-desirability bias
- vague response categories
- overlapping categories
- reference period or item time frame
- not-applicable option
- randomized response scheme (RRS)

The question concerns behavior constrained by strong social norms. Respondents may underreport it to present themselves favorably, creating systematic social-desirability bias. “Seldom,” “occasionally,” and “sometimes” have no shared numerical meanings and may overlap. The question also provides no time period, assumes that every respondent has a child, and assumes a common interpretation of “corporal punishment.”

**A behaviorally specific revision**

> During the past 30 days, how many times did you intentionally spank or strike your child as punishment?
>
> 0 / 1 / 2--3 / 4--5 / 6 or more / I do not have a child

The revision adds a time frame, an observable behavior, mutually exclusive quantity categories, and an applicability option. If truthful responding remains a serious concern, the RRS in Question 4 can exchange greater random variance for stronger individual privacy and potentially less social-desirability bias.

### 1(d) Lying with good intentions

> It is acceptable to lie as long as you have good intentions.

**Course location**

- WK2 pp. 19--20: “Are these good items?”
- WK3 p. 14: social desirability.
- DeVellis & Thorpe, pp. 99--101: ambiguity, conditional wording, and double-barreled items.

**Key terms**

- conditional wording
- construct ambiguity
- social-desirability bias
- content sampling

“Acceptable,” “lie,” and “good intentions” may evoke very different situations across respondents. The phrase “as long as” also binds a judgment about lying to a judgment about motivation. A white lie, temporary withholding of medical information, academic dishonesty, and a lie intended to protect someone are not psychologically equivalent contexts.

**A more specific revision**

> It is acceptable to withhold distressing information temporarily when doing so is intended to protect another person.

Even this item samples only one behavioral context. A measure of general lying permissiveness should contain several clearly worded items covering theoretically relevant contexts, followed by an evaluation of their internal structure.

### 1(e) The extinct-animal item

> An example of an extinct animal is an ...

**Course location**

- WK2 pp. 11, 13: optimal-performance tests and multiple-choice formats.
- WK3 p. 1: correct/incorrect scoring, formula scoring, and partial credit.

**Key terms**

- keyed correct option or answer key
- distractor
- unambiguous key
- content validity

The answer to a knowledge-test item must be defensible and unambiguous. The original alternatives mix broad animal categories with more specific species names. Broad categories may include both living and extinct members, while the status of an obscure species may itself invite disagreement. An incorrect response can therefore reflect an unclear item boundary rather than deficient biology knowledge.

**A more defensible revision**

> Which of the following species is extinct?
>
> A. Dodo
>
> B. Chimpanzee
>
> C. Bald eagle
>
> D. Blue whale

The revised alternatives are at similar taxonomic levels, one option is clearly keyed, and the remaining options are plausible but clearly incorrect distractors.

### 1(f) The U.S.-coin arithmetic item

> You have a quarter, three pennies, two dimes, and four nickels. How much money in total do you have in cents?

**Course location**

- WK1 p. 7: target population and cross-cultural use.
- WK2 p. 4: fairness and construct-irrelevant barriers.
- WK2 p. 10: an item format must be feasible for the intended population.

**Key terms**

- fairness
- target population
- cultural loading
- construct-irrelevant variance

If the target construct is integer addition, the item also requires knowledge of the names and values of U.S. coins. For students unfamiliar with U.S. currency or English coin vocabulary, an error may reflect cultural knowledge rather than arithmetic skill. The resulting score is contaminated by construct-irrelevant variance.

**The appropriate revision depends on the testing purpose**

- If the construct is arithmetic, ask for (25+3+20+20) directly.
- If practical currency use is part of the construct, retain the coins but state that currency identification belongs in the test blueprint and verify that the target population received relevant instruction.
- If language barriers should be minimized, show coin images and values rather than names alone.

## Question 2: An analytic scoring rubric

### 2.1 Analytic versus holistic scoring

**Course location**

- WK1 p. 7: establish the purpose, target population, and latent construct first.
- WK1 p. 10: define the construct domain, blueprint, review, and pilot process.
- WK1 pp. 13--14: content coverage, task analysis, and subject-matter experts.
- WK3 p. 1: scoring is a distinct facet of item and scale development.
- WK3 p. 11: the halo effect.

Holistic scoring asks a rater to form one overall impression. Analytic scoring decomposes performance into explicit dimensions, scores each dimension, and combines the ratings using a prespecified rule. The analytic approach makes the evidence for a decision more transparent and can reduce halo effects: a favorable overall impression should not automatically elevate every component rating.

### 2.2 A three-level analytic rubric

| Criterion | 1 — Needs improvement | 2 — Meets expectations | 3 — Exceeds expectations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Research and conceptual competence | Demonstrates important gaps in theory or literature; the research question is unclear or disconnected from evidence | Accurately explains major theory and formulates a feasible question | Integrates multiple literatures, identifies a meaningful gap, and formulates a valuable new question |
| Methodological competence | Makes consequential design or analysis errors and requires extensive guidance | Selects and implements generally appropriate methods; major claims are supported | Independently justifies methods, checks assumptions, and conducts robustness analyses |
| Professional performance | Frequently misses deadlines; communication, collaboration, or research records are inadequate | Works reliably and on time, communicates clearly, and follows research norms | Proactively advances the work, demonstrates strong independence, and supports the team effectively |

Sum the three ratings to obtain a total from 3 to 9, then map it to three final categories:

- 3--4: Needs improvement
- 5--7: Meets expectations
- 8--9: Exceeds expectations

### 2.3 What else does a defensible rubric require?

- **Behavioral anchors:** Define levels using observable behavior rather than circular labels such as “good” or “average.”
- **Content validity:** Dimensions and weights should follow program goals and faculty subject-matter expertise, not an individual rater's momentary preference.
- **Inter-rater reliability:** Multiple faculty members should practice on common examples and evaluate whether their ratings agree sufficiently.
- **Evidence record:** Each component rating should be supported by concrete evidence.
- **Decision rule:** Category thresholds, treatment of missing evidence, and whether a critical weakness can be compensated by strength elsewhere should be specified in advance.

!!! warning "Three labels are not automatically an analytic rubric"
    A scale containing only “poor / average / good” is still an overall impression. Analytic scoring requires **multiple construct-relevant criteria + behavioral anchors for every level + an explicit aggregation rule**.

## Question 3: Four scoring algorithms for a Mark-M item

### 3.1 Item format and notation

**Course location**

- WK2 p. 15: Pick (K) out of (N) and multiple-mark items.
- WK3 p. 1: Mark-M items, partial credit, and scoring choices.
- WK3 p. 2: eight Mark-M scoring algorithms; the assignment asks the student to select and explain four.

The item contains 10 cities. Santiago, Prague, Berlin, and Cairo are the four keyed correct options. Define:

| Symbol | Meaning | Value or relationship in this item |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (TO) | Total number of options | (10) |
| (CO) | Number of keyed correct options | (4) |
| (MCO=m) | Correct options marked | (0\leq m\leq4) |
| (MIO=i) | Incorrect options marked | (0\leq i\leq6) |
| (CC) | Correct choices, including marked correct options and unmarked distractors | (CC=m+(6-i)) |
| (IC) | Incorrect choices, including marked distractors and unmarked correct options | (IC=i+(4-m)) |

A “choice” here is a binary decision about each option: select or do not select. Consequently, correctly leaving a distractor unmarked counts as a correct option-level decision.

### 3.2 Four algorithms and their measurement meanings

| Algorithm | Definition | What it rewards or penalizes | Advantage | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SA1 Exact match | (S=1) if (IC=0); otherwise (S=0) | Credit only for a completely correct response pattern | Simple, strict, and resistant to partial-credit strategies | Omitting one correct answer produces zero; little information is retained |
| SA2 Correct minus incorrect | (S=(CC-IC)/TO) | Rewards correct decisions and symmetrically penalizes incorrect decisions | Distinguishes partial knowledge from total failure | Negative scores are possible; strategic behavior can be complex with unequal key proportions |
| SA3 Proportion of correct decisions | (S=CC/TO) | Weights every option-level decision equally | Intuitive and bounded between 0 and 1 | When distractors are numerous, leaving everything unmarked can earn a high score |
| SA4 Hit rate minus false-positive rate | (S=MCO/CO-MIO/(TO-CO)) | Balances correct detection against false selection | Prevents the larger distractor set from dominating the score | May be negative and is less immediately intuitive than a percentage |

SA4 has the structure of sensitivity minus false-positive rate:

\[
S_{SA4}
=
\frac{\text{correct options selected}}{\text{all correct options}}
-
\frac{\text{incorrect options selected}}{\text{all incorrect options}}.
\]

It distinguishes the ability to identify correct options from the tendency to falsely identify distractors as correct.

### 3.3 Apply all four algorithms to one's own response

Substituting (TO=10) and (CO=4):

\[
S_{SA1}=
\begin{cases}
1,&m=4\text{ and }i=0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

\[
S_{SA2}=\frac{m-i+1}{5},
\qquad
S_{SA3}=\frac{m+6-i}{10},
\qquad
S_{SA4}=\frac{m}{4}-\frac{i}{6}.
\]

For example, suppose a respondent marks three correct cities, omits Cairo, and marks no distractor. Then (m=3,i=0):

| Algorithm | Score |
| --- | ---: |
| SA1 | (0) |
| SA2 | (0.80) |
| SA3 | (0.90) |
| SA4 | (0.75) |

The different scores show that a scoring rule is not merely a technical afterthought. It operationally defines which response pattern counts as mastery.

## Question 4: Randomized response scheme

### 4.1 Why introduce randomization?

**Course location**

- WK3 p. 10: measurement error versus systematic bias.
- WK3 p. 14: social desirability.
- WK3 p. 15: the loaded-coin RRS example.
- WK3 pp. 16--17: the estimator and worked example.
- WK3 p. 18: failure with a fair coin, a small sample, or an individual-level objective.

Direct questions about cheating, bullying, or other sensitive behaviors may lead to systematic underreporting. A randomized response scheme prevents the researcher from knowing whether any particular response reveals the individual's true status, while the known randomization probability still permits estimation of the population prevalence.

The design trades two sources of error:

- **Systematic bias** pushes estimates in a direction and does not cancel through repetition.
- **Random measurement or sampling error** fluctuates across samples and can cancel on average, but it reduces precision.

RRS deliberately accepts additional random variance in exchange for privacy and potentially less social-desirability bias.

### 4.2 Probability model and sampling distribution

Let:

- (pi) be the population prevalence of having cheated;
- (p=0.7) be the probability of heads, under which the respondent answers truthfully;
- (1-p=0.3) be the probability of tails, under which the respondent answers untruthfully;
- (lambda=P(\text{observed Yes})).

An observed Yes occurs through one of two mutually exclusive paths: a person who has cheated gets heads and answers truthfully, or a person who has not cheated gets tails and reverses the truthful answer. Therefore,

\[
\lambda=p\pi+(1-p)(1-\pi).
\]

Each observed response (X_j) follows a Bernoulli distribution, and the total number of Yes responses follows a binomial distribution:

\[
X_j\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\lambda),
\qquad
Y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_j\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,\lambda).
\]

Solving the first equation for (pi) gives

\[
\hat\pi
=
\frac{\hat\lambda+p-1}{2p-1},
\qquad
\hat\lambda=\frac Yn.
\]

The denominator (2p-1) explains why this version fails with a fair coin. When (p=0.5), the denominator is zero and the observed Yes probability no longer identifies (pi).

### 4(a) Eighty Yes responses among 120 students

\[
\hat\lambda=\frac{80}{120}=\frac23.
\]

\[
\hat\pi
=
\frac{\frac23+0.7-1}{2(0.7)-1}
=
\frac{0.3667}{0.4}
\approx0.9167.
\]

The estimated prevalence of ever having cheated is approximately (91.67\%\). The observed proportion (80/120) is not itself the target prevalence: it combines truthful responses with forced reversals.

### 4(b) Eight Yes responses among 12 students

Because (8/12=2/3), the point estimate remains (91.67\%\). The sample, however, is only one tenth as large and the estimate is much less stable.

\[
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\pi)
=
\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{n(2p-1)^2},
\]

\[
SE(\hat\pi)
=
\sqrt{\frac{\hat\lambda(1-\hat\lambda)}{n(2p-1)^2}}.
\]

With the same observed proportion, reducing (n) from 120 to 12 multiplies the standard error by (sqrt{10}). The estimate in part (a) is therefore more precise.

### 4(c) Direct questioning

Under direct questioning, an observed Yes is treated as a truthful Yes:

\[
\hat\pi_{\text{direct}}=\frac{80}{120}=0.6667.
\]

This is lower than the RRS estimate of (0.9167). The comparison does not prove that the RRS value is the truth. The conceptual point is that direct questioning has less sampling variance but may suffer from social-desirability bias, whereas RRS increases variance while protecting individual status and attempting to reduce systematic underreporting.

### 4(d) Sample size for equal precision

The plug-in variance of the direct estimator is

\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\pi_D)
=
\frac{(2/3)(1/3)}{120}
=
\frac{1}{540}.
\]

If a future RRS sample uses (hat\lambda=2/3), then

\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\pi_R)
=
\frac{(2/3)(1/3)}{n(0.4)^2}
=
\frac{25}{18n}.
\]

Requiring the RRS variance to be no larger than the direct-question variance yields

\[
\frac{25}{18n}\leq\frac{1}{540}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
n\geq750.
\]

Thus, under the plug-in comparison based on the observed proportions in the question, at least (750) participants are required.

!!! note "The sample-size answer depends on the comparison assumption"
    The value (750) uses the observed (2/3) in each design as its plug-in probability. If the comparison instead holds a common true prevalence (pi=2/3) across designs, the RRS observation probability is (lambda=0.3+0.4(2/3)=17/30), producing a minimum of (829) after rounding up. A complete response should state the assumption; (750) is the most direct answer under the proportions supplied in the assignment.

### 4.3 When should this version not be used?

- (p=0.5): the prevalence is not identifiable.
- The sample is small: randomization makes the estimator unstable and an unconstrained estimate may even fall outside ([0,1]).
- The objective is an individual decision: RRS intentionally hides individual status and is designed for group prevalence.
- Respondents misunderstand or fail to follow the randomization instructions: the assumed response mechanism is broken and the estimator can be biased.

## Chinese-English terminology guide

| 中文 | English | Role in this assignment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 项目／题目 | item | The smallest measurement stimulus and response unit |
| 目标构念 | target construct | The psychological attribute, knowledge, or ability the score claims to represent |
| 构念无关方差 | construct-irrelevant variance | Score variation caused by language, cultural knowledge, or complex wording rather than the target construct |
| 双重问题 | double-barreled item | One stem asks the respondent to judge two claims |
| 社会赞许性偏差 | social-desirability bias | Responses are systematically altered to create a favorable impression |
| 反应量表 | response scale | The set of options used to express an answer or degree |
| 行为锚点 | behavioral anchor | An observable behavior used to define a rating level |
| 分析式评分 | analytic scoring | Separate dimension scores are combined using a rule |
| 整体式评分 | holistic scoring | One score or category is assigned from an overall impression |
| 光环效应 | halo effect | A global impression systematically influences component judgments |
| 评分者间信度 | inter-rater reliability | Consistency of ratings across raters |
| 部分计分 | partial credit | A partially correct response receives some credit |
| 正确命中率 | hit rate or sensitivity | Proportion of correct options successfully selected |
| 假阳性率 | false-positive rate | Proportion of incorrect options incorrectly selected |
| 系统偏差 | systematic bias | Directional error that accumulates rather than canceling over repeated measurement |
| 随机误差 | random error | Fluctuation whose direction varies across repeated samples |
| 随机化反应方案 | randomized response scheme | A known random mechanism protects individual privacy while supporting population estimation |
| 流行率／总体比例 | prevalence or population proportion | The target parameter (pi) in Question 4 |
| 精度 | precision | Sampling stability, commonly summarized by variance or standard error |
